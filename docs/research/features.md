# Fix reconnection and outages before adding features

The portal you have already built covers the only self-service tasks with mass, recurring, every-customer demand — and that is not an accident of luck, it is where the evidence points. What it is missing is not more billing screens. The largest documented call driver at fixed-broadband providers is service state, not money: coded open-ended responses on the actual reason for a customer's most recent support call put **internet outage at 31% and technical issues at 22% for cable/internet, with billing questions a distant 12%** ([MeasuringU](https://measuringu.com/customer-service/)). Against that, Gartner's survey of 5,728 customers found only **14% of issues are fully resolved in self-service**, rising to **36% for issues customers rate "very simple"** ([Gartner, Aug 2024](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2024-08-19-gartner-survey-finds-only-14-percent-of-customer-service-issues-are-fully-resolved-in-self-service)) — which means a portal only earns its keep on transactional, unambiguous tasks and actively annoys people when it attempts diagnosis or negotiation. Three things follow for ISPMan. First, the next build should be the prepaid service-state loop — days remaining, pay, back online immediately, confirmed by SMS — because for a prepaid customer the question is never "what do I owe?" but "how long do I have left and how do I get back online right now?" Second, a suspended customer frequently cannot reach a portal at all, so the reconnection path has to live outside the login, in a walled garden, the way UISP and Visp.net both implement it ([UISP help](https://help.uisp.com/hc/en-us/articles/22590998643351-UISP-CRM-Prepaid-Service-and-Service-Reactivation); [Visp.net](https://visp.net/?p=459)). Third, outage communication is simultaneously the biggest call driver and a **documented hole in the small-ISP platform market** — Splynx operators have had an open request for a customer-portal network-status module since July 2022, answered only by another operator who built a custom app ([Splynx forum](https://forum.splynx.com/t/network-status-module-on-customer-portal-app/2262)) — which makes it the one place a one-person platform can beat the incumbent vendors outright. Be warned that much of what follows rests on thin evidence: the research turned up almost no measured data on what customers actually do inside an ISP portal, and the few quantified deflection claims in this category are vendor-published single cases.

## The evidence is strong on what customers call about and weak on everything vendors sell

Two bodies of evidence exist here, and they barely overlap. On one side sit genuinely measured findings with stated methods: the Gartner resolution rates above; **J.D. Power's 2026 U.S. Telecom Digital Experience Study** of 12,082 customers fielded December 2025–January 2026, which scores ISP digital experience at 659 and names changing account information or settings among the tasks customers find *harder* online ([Businesswire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260318404559/en/Telecom-Provider-Mobile-Apps-Deliver-Strong-Customer-Experience-but-Websites-Fall-Short-JD-Power-Finds)); the **NRTC 2024 benchmarking medians across 84 rural US broadband providers**, showing 41% of customers on autopay, 48% on paperless billing, 50% having downloaded the provider app, help-desk first-call resolution of 75%, and **truck rolls at 28% of tickets costing US$130 each** ([NRTC 2024 PDF](https://www.nrtc.coop/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/Rural-Broadband-Operations-Benchmarking-Report-2024.pdf)); and a US Bank survey of 1,049 US adults aged 25+ fielded January 2020, in which 64% were registered on a utility or telecom portal, paying bills and checking balances were the main uses, **in-portal payment reminders were used by only 15%** and recurring payments by 24% ([US Bank 2020 PDF](https://www.usbank.com/dam/financialiq/documents/utilities-and-telecom-consumer-payment-insight-report-2020.pdf)). That last source is the best available proxy for portal task mix and it is six years old, US-only, and bundles utilities with telecom so the sectors cannot be separated.

On the other side sits the feature list every billing vendor advertises — chatbots, in-portal speed tests, router management, plan comparison, loyalty, live chat, usage dashboards — for which the research found **essentially no measured usage data at all**. The most widely circulated number in this space, "self-service portal reduces ISP support tickets by 40%", is bylined by an editorial team, contains no percentages, sample sizes or before/after results, and only describes what proof a rollout *should* produce ([ISPbills](https://ispbills.com/blog/customer-portal-self-service-isp/)). No vendor in the set examined — Splynx, Sonar, UISP, Powercode, Visp.net, Azotel, Rev.io — publishes a quantified support-call reduction metric, and the one case study explicitly titled around call reduction contains no percentage ([CaseStudies.com, Plains Internet / Sonar](https://www.casestudies.com/company/sonar-broadband-ossbss/case-study/plains-internet-reduces-support-calls-with-sonar)). Splynx's own self-care article is qualitative throughout; the only figure in it is an unrelated McKinsey correlation between satisfaction and revenue which Splynx does not tie to its own results ([Splynx](https://splynx.com/blog/customer-service/shift-towards-user-self-care-leveraging-splynx-customer-portal-tr-069-acs/)). Searches for telecom chatbot containment returned only vendor content asserting 70–90% with no methodology, and the researchers deliberately declined to report those as benchmarks. Treat that vacuum as the finding it is.

The sharpest measured datapoint in the whole corpus is a negative one: **roughly 50% of companies offer live chat and e-forums, and fewer than 2% of customers use them**, while only about 3% of customers use a digital service channel in a given month against roughly 6% for the call centre, and e-care satisfaction runs almost 10% *lower* than traditional channels ([McKinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/growth-marketing-and-sales/our-insights/overcoming-obstacles-to-digital-customer-care)). The same McKinsey research carries the warning that should govern this entire project: some companies that moved to digital **saw call volume rise instead**, because the digital experience did not resolve the issue, and at one company **about 40% of e-care users eventually called the call centre anyway**. Its headline upside — digital care cutting call-centre volume 25–30% — comes from tier-1 European operators and is not independently verifiable from the article. A small WISP should plan for the downside case as the default.

One further asymmetry decides what is worth building. Billing *disputes* barely deflect: **78% of customers with a billing dispute call customer service** ([US Bank 2020](https://www.usbank.com/dam/financialiq/documents/utilities-and-telecom-consumer-payment-insight-report-2020.pdf)). A disputing customer wants a human to agree with them, and no screen substitutes. The portal's billing job is therefore prevention rather than resolution: an unambiguous ledger showing what was charged, what was received and when service expires, so the dispute never forms. Meanwhile, with 28% of tickets ending in a US$130 truck roll, a feature that avoids one site visit is worth far more to a small operator than one that avoids a phone call — which is the strongest economic argument for the self-diagnosis and service-state features below, and against the cosmetic ones.

## The prepaid loop is about time remaining, and Splynx is the only full reference

Prepaid self-care is structurally different from postpaid and most platforms handle it badly. The honest starting point is that **no measured breakdown of prepaid self-care tasks exists** — the researchers searched GSMA, Analysys Mason and operator material and found nothing quantifying balance versus top-up versus expiry versus reconnect, and named this the single largest evidence gap in the assignment. What follows is therefore a reading of the available implementations plus reasoning, not measurement.

Splynx is the reference because it is the only platform in the set combining all three prepaid shapes. It has a genuine prepaid billing type set per customer, with a configurable **"minimal balance" at which access is blocked, defaulting to zero**, and a daily deduction: the documented example divides a US$40 monthly plan across a 30-day month at roughly US$1.33 per day, so a US$20 deposit lasts about 15 days before the balance hits zero and the customer is blocked ([Splynx](https://splynx.com/blog/billing/prepaid-billing-in-splynx)). It has customer-initiated top-ups whose restore behaviour is the single most important design detail in this whole report — when a customer buys a top-up, "customer's data will be immediately increased and access to the Internet is restored" ([Splynx](https://splynx.com/blog/billing/data-top-ups/)). And it has RADIUS hotspot vouchers that are usable the moment they are generated, with no separate activation step ([Splynx wiki](https://wiki.splynx.com/customer_management/prepaid_vouchers)). Two caveats the researchers flagged: Splynx's prepaid article never states whether customers can fund their own deposit — the portal overview separately confirms customers can top up their account balance online, so the two sources together imply yes, but no single source says it for the prepaid billing type — and the voucher documentation never describes the customer-side redemption flow at all.

What the others do is weaker. UISP is not prepaid in the airtime sense: it is postpaid invoicing with a customer-driven pay-ahead, where paying several months at once marks the first invoice paid and keeps the excess as credit that later invoices draw on automatically, provided forward invoicing and automatic credit use are both enabled ([UISP](https://help.uisp.com/hc/en-us/articles/22590998643351-UISP-CRM-Prepaid-Service-and-Service-Reactivation)). Sonar's nearest equivalent is mid-cycle Data Top-Off against a cap ([Sonar KB](https://sonarsoftware.helpjuice.com/getting-started/how-to-using-sonars-customer-portal)). Splynx's and Sonar's top-ups both buy prepaid *data*, not prepaid *time* — a different product from "buy thirty days of service" — and **no vendor in the research documents a literal "buy 7 days" purchase in the portal**. ISPMan's calendar-month prepaid model therefore has no off-the-shelf precedent to copy, which cuts both ways: there is no template, and no competitor has one either.

A prepaid self-care screen, then, should open on time rather than money. Days remaining as the dominant element, the expiry date underneath it, one sentence saying what happens at expiry, and one button to extend. Balance matters only where it is a stored value the customer can draw down; on calendar-month prepaid, a balance figure invites the "what do I owe?" framing that does not apply. Behind the button, the only thing that matters is that paying restores access in seconds without a human in the loop — a reconnection-latency problem in the RADIUS and payment-webhook integration, not a UI problem. There is **no measured data on how long a prepaid customer will wait after paying before calling**, but the structural argument is straightforward: prepaid payment events recur monthly or more often, so the volume of "did it land, am I back on?" contacts is inherently higher than postpaid, and an automatic SMS receipt plus instant reconnection plausibly deflects more contacts than any portal screen. Flagged as inference, not finding.

## A suspended customer cannot reach your portal, so reconnection lives outside it

This is the structural flaw in the portal as currently conceived, and the research is unusually clear about it. Both platforms that document reconnection implement it as a **walled-garden page, not a button inside an authenticated portal**. UISP redirects suspended clients to a suspension page where the client selects the months to pay and completes payment through the gateway; this requires the suspension feature and client-zone reactivation to both be enabled, and an "Active to" date to be set ([UISP](https://help.uisp.com/hc/en-us/articles/22590998643351-UISP-CRM-Prepaid-Service-and-Service-Reactivation)). Visp.net's Customer Self-Management Portal does the same: suspended subscribers are taken to a payment screen and can reconnect once their account is up to date ([Visp.net](https://visp.net/?p=459)). Notably, **UISP's flow requires no portal login at all** — it is triggered by the act of browsing — which makes it the lowest-friction reconnect mechanism found anywhere in this research. One caveat the researchers were careful to preserve: UISP's own documentation does not state when service is actually restored after a walled-garden payment, and the article advises confirming that behaviour in your own setup or with Ubiquiti support.

For ISPMan this is mostly a configuration problem rather than a new system, because the three pieces needed already exist. FreeRADIUS can return a restricted profile for an expired customer instead of rejecting the session outright, so the subscriber stays connected but constrained. MikroTik, reachable over the existing WireGuard tunnels, can hold that profile's address list and redirect its HTTP traffic to the tenant's reconnection page while permitting the destinations that page needs — the tenant subdomain, the Fygaro endpoints, and whatever CDN assets are involved. And because the identity of the session is already known to RADIUS, the reconnection page can be pre-filled with the right account rather than asking a cut-off customer to log in. That last point is where the per-customer MAC records earn their place: a MAC or RADIUS session attribute is enough to resolve the account and mint a tokenised pay link, which is the same shape as Centipid's payment links that sign the user straight into an invoice and remain valid for seven days ([Centipid](https://docs.centipidbilling.com/customer-portal/overview)). The design is the author's own engineering judgement; the research documents the pattern on UISP and Visp.net but contains nothing on MikroTik or FreeRADIUS implementation specifics.

Three constraints deserve stating plainly. The walled garden must permit the payment gateway's domains or the flow dies at the checkout step — the Jamaican version of the complaint that **topping up a Digicel account requires Wi-Fi once credit runs out** ([App Store, MyDigicel](https://apps.apple.com/app/id1039460951)), which is the same dead end in a different form: a service that needs connectivity to buy connectivity. Multi-tenancy makes this harder than it looks, since each tenant's gateway credentials and reconnection page differ, so the allow-list is per-tenant configuration rather than a global rule. And the garden has to cover the customer who pays at a till rather than online: when staff record a cash payment, the same reconnection path must fire automatically, which is a ledger-event subscription rather than a payment-gateway webhook. Build it as one event, two triggers.

## Outage communication is the open gap and the cheapest credibility you can buy

Here the opportunity and the evidence line up. The gap is real and documented: Splynx operators raised a feature request for a network-status module in the customer portal in **July 2022**, explicitly noting that the networking module already monitors devices, and two years later the only answer in the thread came from another operator who had built a custom app to push outage notifications ([Splynx forum](https://forum.splynx.com/t/network-status-module-on-customer-portal-app/2262)). UISP's client-zone outage capability could not be established from available documentation. Sonar's blog lists "real-time network status checks" among self-service capabilities but **does not clearly attribute them to Sonar's own portal**, and the researchers judged them to read as category aspirations rather than shipped features ([Sonar](https://www.sonar.software/blog/reduce-support-ticket-volume-and-empower-subscribers-with-self-service-tools)). Splynx does ship outage, maintenance and product-update messages in the portal and asserts they reduce support contacts, with no number attached ([Splynx](https://splynx.com/blog/customer-service/customer-portal-overview/)).

In Jamaica this is not merely a differentiator, it is a regulatory expectation. The **OUR has directed providers to immediately notify customers of service interruptions and expected restoration times**, said it would explore ways to incentivise faster resolution, and said future ICT legislation should include powers to impose and enforce sanctions for customer-service breaches ([Jamaica Information Service](https://jis.gov.jm/govt-to-work-with-telecoms-stakeholders-to-address-service-challenges/)). A tenant ISP that can show per-customer outage notification has a compliance story the national carriers have struggled to deliver.

The quantified evidence is thin and must be labelled as such. The single attributable ISP number is a **26% reduction in inbound technical support calls at ETC Communications** after automating outage notification, achieved despite 6% subscriber growth and without adding customer-service staff — and it is vendor-published, a single case, not independently verified, and measures technical calls specifically, which is precisely where outage calls live ([Lightwave/GOCare](https://www.lightwaveonline.com/broadband/article/55403765/gocare-outageiq)). An adjacent-sector claim of a 57% call-centre reduction during public safety power shutoffs is electricity utilities and also vendor-published ([GoConvey](https://www.goconvey.com/how-proactive-communication-reduced-call-center-volume-by-57-during-public-safety-power-shutoffs/)). The cleanest causal evidence in the entire research set is more modest: a champion/challenger study at a telecom client with roughly 50 million subscribers found that customers who opted in to outage SMS alerts showed a **3% reduction in the two-hour repeat call rate and a 13% increase in IVR automation**, statistically significant by two-sample t-test — but total inbound call reduction was not quantified, no sample sizes were given, and the paper dates from 2017 ([SAS Global Forum 2017 PDF](https://support.sas.com/resources/papers/proceedings17/1328-2017.pdf)). That 3% is a useful corrective to vendor enthusiasm, and its mechanism is exactly right: customers stop re-calling to ask whether it is fixed yet.

The minimal version is small. An incident record with an affected-scope field, a status and an update timestamp; a banner on the dashboard shown only to subscribers inside that scope; an SMS on open and another on restore; and a straight answer in the fault-report flow to the only question driving the call, which is "is this me or you?" Scoping matters far more than presentation — a global "we have an outage" banner shown to unaffected customers generates calls rather than preventing them — and this is where a small WISP has a structural advantage over a large one, because ISPMan already knows which sector, AP or tunnel each subscriber sits behind. A public geospatial outage map is the last thing to build, not the first: a map tells the customer where the problem is, which they do not care about, while the banner tells them whether it is their problem, which is the only thing they called to find out.

One design caution. **GOCare, the most mature vendor in this niche, ships restoration confirmations but its write-up does not mention estimated restoration times** ([Lightwave](https://www.lightwaveonline.com/broadband/article/55403765/gocare-outageiq)). The likely reason is that a missed ETR generates a second call, and the OUR's directive to state expected restoration times sits in tension with that. The safer pattern is a last-updated and next-update-by timestamp rather than a promised fix time, which satisfies the spirit of the directive without creating a broken promise. The research found **no evidence either way on whether displaying ETR helps or hurts**, so treat this as reasoned preference.

## Proactive SMS outperforms a portal people must remember to visit

This matters more here than it would elsewhere, because the portal is not competing with a rival portal — it is competing with walking to the till. The structural case for notifications is built from several measured pieces. Portal visit frequency clusters around **monthly: 34% of registered users visit 10–14 times a year and 30% visit only one to four times**, consistent with a billing-cycle-driven pattern ([US Bank 2020](https://www.usbank.com/dam/financialiq/documents/utilities-and-telecom-consumer-payment-insight-report-2020.pdf)). Push reach is a precondition most operators never meet: **single-channel bill receipt via mobile app notification is just 8%, and via text also 8%**. Bill delivery channel measurably changes payment speed, with 35% saying delivery method affects how quickly they pay and 26% saying digital delivery speeds it up. And roughly a third of paper retention is operator-caused rather than customer preference — 14% do not know digital options exist, 14% say email statements are not offered, 6% say text is not offered.

Put beside the SAS outage-SMS result and the 5–6% app adoption figures discussed below, the conclusion is that for a customer who opens a portal ten times a year, **a well-timed SMS is the self-service channel and the portal is the ledger of record behind it**. The researchers were explicit that this is an inference from the combination of visit frequency, 8% notification reach and the SAS result — **no study was found comparing notification-led against portal-led self-care by ARPU or income segment**, in either direction. The practical consequence is nonetheless clear and cheap: ISPMan's SMS queue, not its portal, should carry the expiry warning, the payment receipt, the outage open and restore, and the reconnection confirmation. Every one of those messages should contain a tokenised link back into the portal, so the customer never has to remember a URL or navigate a login — the link *is* the entry point. And the defaults should be on, because the lesson of 15% uptake on in-portal payment reminders is that a useful notification left as an opt-in setting simply does not get switched on.

One anti-pattern to avoid: a convenience fee on digital payment directly undermines the deflection case, and the research shows some consumers will switch payment method to avoid one ([US Bank 2020](https://www.usbank.com/dam/financialiq/documents/utilities-and-telecom-consumer-payment-insight-report-2020.pdf)). Charging customers to use the channel that saves the operator money is self-defeating. For contrast, note which way GraceKennedy priced the same decision, below.

## Jamaica trains customers to pay at a counter and to expect Flow's app

Customer expectations in this market are set entirely by two national carriers, because there is no local peer: the one notable independent Jamaican wireless ISP, **Dekal Wireless, was acquired by Cable & Wireless in 2014 and its network decommissioned by Flow in 2022** ([Jamaica Gleaner, 2014](https://jamaica-gleaner.com/article/business/20141210/cwc-company-acquires-small-wifi-rival-dekal); [Jamaica Gleaner, 2022](https://jamaica-gleaner.com/article/business/20220701/flow-decommissions-dekal-wireless-network)). The researchers found no evidence of any smaller Jamaican ISP or WISP running a documented self-service portal at all, and treated that as a real finding rather than missing data.

The high bar is Flow's app, and it is genuinely high: **rated 4.6 out of 5 from 4.1 thousand ratings**, with bill payment, PDF bill download, autopay management, prepaid top-up, real-time usage tracking, plan and add-on purchase, modem reset, outage checks, in-app chat and push notifications ([App Store, My Flow](https://apps.apple.com/app/id958379748)). Flow has offered **remote reset of cable modems and set-top boxes since 2018, across all 15 Flow markets** ([Cable & Wireless press release](https://www.cwc.com/news-and-media/press-releases/flow-customers-get-more-convenient-new-features-with-latest-release-of-myflow-app.html)) — so Jamaican expectations are shaped by a regional product, not a Jamaica-specific one. The low bar is Digicel: **2.8 out of 5 from 2.3 thousand ratings**, home broadband absent from the app description entirely, and reviews reporting that bill payment is not possible in the app and that purchases stall at the payment step ([App Store, MyDigicel](https://apps.apple.com/app/id1039460951)). Digicel's own Jamaica LTE Home terms route **every** service change — modem relocation, termination — to a phone call or email ([Digicel JM T&Cs](https://www.digicelgroup.com/jm/en/legal/lte-home-terms-and-conditions-digicel-plus)). Both carriers' app complaints converge on the same theme, and it is not missing features: inaccurate balances needing frequent refreshing, top-ups not displaying, usage and balance data slow to update, credits unresolved for a week. The gap to exploit is data trustworthiness, not feature count.

How people actually pay is the constraint that should shape the portal most. Jamaica runs on a bill-payment-agent model. Digicel+ accepts payment at Digicel stores, in the MyDigicel app, at **PayMaster and Prime Trust**, and through **Jamaica National, Bill Express, Sagicor Online, Scotia Online and NCB Online** ([Digicel JM T&Cs](https://www.digicelgroup.com/jm/en/legal/lte-home-terms-and-conditions-digicel-plus)). The electricity utility publishes an even longer agent list alongside its app and web portal, and mentions no WhatsApp, SMS, mobile wallet or direct debit at all ([JPS](https://jpsco.com/payment-billing)). Bill Express reaches into rural areas through **39 post offices** ([Jamaica Information Service](https://jis.gov.jm/bill-express-services-now-available-39-post-offices/)). The 2025 development worth watching is **GraceKennedy's Bill Express WhatsApp Pay**, which takes bill payments and mobile top-ups inside WhatsApp for Flow, JPS and NWC at **J$55 per transaction, priced explicitly below the in-store rate**, accepting local and overseas cards and aimed partly at the diaspora ([Jamaica Observer, 22 Oct 2025](https://www.jamaicaobserver.com/2025/10/22/gk-eyes-digital-growth-bill-express-whatsapp-pay/)). GraceKennedy is deliberately pricing customers off the counter and into chat — the opposite of a convenience fee — and declined to give transaction figures, so uptake is unknown. Note also that **Paymaster is controlled by Digicel** ([Nearshore Americas](https://nearshoreamericas.com/digicel-takes-control-jamaicas-paymaster-hopes-launch-mobile-wallet/)), meaning a tenant WISP competing with Digicel may be routing payments through its rival's rail. Crucially, **no source quantifies the method mix** — what share of Jamaican ISP bills are paid cash-at-agent versus online card versus bank transfer versus WhatsApp. Every claim of dominance here is directional inference from which channels providers publish, not measured share.

Three design consequences follow. First, the portal must treat payments that arrive through an agent as first-class, not as an exception the admin reconciles later: the till payment and the Fygaro payment should produce the same ledger event, the same SMS receipt, and the same reconnection. Digicel's instruction to quote the LTE Home account number on every payment is a tell that its third-party reconciliation is loosely coupled and payments arrive as unmatched deposits — exactly the problem ISPMan will face, which is why the account number belongs prominently on the portal's main screen for the customer to read out at a counter. Second, payment-posting latency is financially material to customers in this market: Digicel charges a **J$250 plus tax late fee** if payment arrives after the 29th on a bill due the 27th, and suspension costs **J$900 plus tax per month** ([Digicel JM T&Cs](https://www.digicelgroup.com/jm/en/legal/lte-home-terms-and-conditions-digicel-plus)). "Has my payment landed?" must be instantly and correctly answerable. Third, WhatsApp is where Jamaican customers already start: Flow customers describe using it for support and complain about its latency ([Trustpilot](https://es.trustpilot.com/review/discoverflow.co)), and a national aggregator has built a payment product inside it. Treat the portal as a link people are sent, not a destination they navigate to. For anything needing a delivery record, note that Digicel's own terms name handset message, email and website posting as its notification channels — **SMS for the record, WhatsApp for the conversation**.

Two sourcing caveats to carry. Flow's own site and both Google Play listings were **blocked by the fetch tool's permission gate** and could not be retrieved, so the feature lists above come from Apple App Store listings and a press release, and the Android review corpus — the more representative one in a market with roughly 62% Android share — is entirely missing. And Flow's Trustpilot profile, scoring **1.2 out of 5 from 168 reviews**, is unclaimed and not invitation-based, so it is a complaint sample rather than a satisfaction measure.

## Phone-number login needs an identifier that is not a phone number

The mechanics of SMS OTP are well documented and cheap to get right. The recommended markup is a real `<input type="text" inputmode="numeric" autocomplete="one-time-code" pattern="\d{6}" required>` inside a real `<form>` — and the form with a real input is described as **"the most important practice"**, because it works in all browsers and lets users type the code when autofill fails. Avoid `type="number"`, which adds spinners and can strip leading zeros, and avoid `type="tel"` as a numeric-keyboard hack, since `inputmode` replaces that trick. The origin-bound SMS format puts optional human-readable text first, then a final line reading `@your-domain.com #123456`, with the domain matching the invoking site and the **total message at 140 characters or fewer** — which both reduces phishing risk, because the suggestion appears only on the matching domain, and lets browsers extract the code reliably. The WebOTP API requires HTTPS, should be feature-detected with `'OTPCredential' in window`, and needs an `AbortController` so it cancels when the user submits manually; it is supported on Chrome, Opera and Vivaldi for Android ([web.dev: SMS OTP form best practices](https://web.dev/articles/sms-otp-form)). Two inconsistencies in Google's own guidance are worth knowing about: the Safari version floor for `autocomplete="one-time-code"` is given as 12+ on one page and 14+ on another, and web.dev's autofill page shows a one-time-code example using `autocomplete="off"` without reconciling it against the documented `one-time-code` value ([web.dev: Autofill](https://web.dev/learn/forms/autofill)).

The identity model is the hard part, and Google's guidance is blunt about it: SMS OTP is "not the most secure method of authentication by itself" because **numbers can be recycled or hijacked** and OTPs are not phishing-resistant ([web.dev](https://web.dev/articles/sms-otp-form)). Corroborating material on number recycling came from a single niche publication whose article bodies the researchers did not fetch and verify, so treat it as signal rather than authority ([NHI](https://nhimg.org/articles/recycled-phone-numbers-expose-the-weakness-in-phone-based-identity/)). The consequence for ISPMan is a data-model decision best made now: the account's primary key must be an internal customer ID, with the phone number as a *verified contact method* attached to it — changeable and re-verifiable — never the identifier itself. That single decision handles recycled numbers, customers who change numbers, and portability in one move.

Then design explicitly for the many-to-many case, because it is the normal case for a small ISP rather than an edge case: one number is often the contact for several accounts, such as a landlord with multiple units or a parent paying for a child's connection, and one account is often reachable on several numbers in a shared household. The login flow therefore needs an **account-chooser step after OTP verification** whenever the verified number maps to more than one account, or the customer silently lands in the wrong one. The shared-phone problem is the most under-appreciated pitfall here: if the household phone authenticates the account, anyone holding it can pause the service, change settings or read the billing history. The mitigation is tiering — OTP alone for read-only views and fault reporting, a second check such as an account PIN or re-verification before destructive or financially significant actions. **No source documented either the account chooser or this tiering for any real ISP portal**, so both are design recommendations rather than observed standards. The same logic extends to mediated use, which is a first-class scenario in a low-literacy context: devices are often shared within families and **infomediaries read messages, navigate interfaces and troubleshoot** on someone else's behalf ([ICTworks](https://www.ictworks.org/traits-low-literacy-technology-users/)), so showing the account holder's name and address on screen lets an intermediary confirm they are in the right account, while genuinely sensitive data stays behind the step-up check.

Two operational points. Always ship the manual-entry path, a resend with a visible countdown, and a fallback channel — voice call, email, or, uniquely available to an ISP that already has a messaging relationship, WhatsApp. And rate-limit the send-OTP endpoint as a cost control, not just a security measure: an unprotected endpoint is an SMS-pumping target that bills the ISP directly, so per-number and per-IP caps, exponential resend backoff, a cap on verification attempts per code, short code lifetimes and geographic restriction all matter. The research deliberately gives **no specific numbers** for code length, expiry window, attempt limits or rate-limit thresholds, because Twilio's documentation was blocked by the fetch tool — the blocked URLs are https://www.twilio.com/docs/verify/best-practices and https://www.twilio.com/docs/verify/preventing-toll-fraud — and the researchers refused to infer them. Source those before setting them. There is likewise **no recommended session lifetime** in any source for a portal of this kind, and **no data on OTP delivery failure rates in emerging markets**, which is the number that would justify the cost of fallback channels.

On the front end, the one concrete, primary-source performance budget worth designing against is Google's example product-page budget: **time to interactive under 5 seconds on slow 3G, first contentful paint under 2 seconds on slow 3G, a Lighthouse performance score above 80, and total JavaScript under 170 KB** ([web.dev: How to stay fast](https://web.dev/how-to-stay-fast/)). That page deliberately sets no total page-weight limit and no low-end-device figure, so the JavaScript budget is the only real size constraint — do not quote a round page-weight number as fact, because none of the sources provides one. The 170 KB ceiling effectively rules out shipping an SPA framework plus a component library plus a charting library to the login screen; render server-side or as static HTML with progressive enhancement and lazy-load the usage chart, the only genuinely JavaScript-hungry component. Design for the fault case as the primary scenario, since a customer with a working connection rarely opens an ISP portal: the whole app will be loaded over cellular on a low-end Android phone while the fixed line is down. Keep the dashboard flat and single-screen with every common task as a visible labelled card rather than a nested menu, since low-literate users **find information hierarchies harder to navigate** and are easily confused by unfamiliar interfaces, and are often **"scared of touching the tech for fear of breaking it"** ([ICTworks](https://www.ictworks.org/traits-low-literacy-technology-users/)) — which makes plain-language confirmation on every state-changing action a feature rather than an obstacle. Splynx's answer to the same problem, large icon-based controls in clear navigable blocks ([Splynx](https://splynx.com/blog/customer-service/customer-portal-overview/)), is convergent evidence for the same layout, but read "icon-based" as a large tappable card containing an icon *and* a short plain-language label, never an unlabelled glyph. Finally, on tap-target sizing: the commonly cited WCAG figures of 24×24 CSS pixels for SC 2.5.8 and 44×44 for SC 2.5.5 **could not be verified against a primary W3C source** — the researchers' attempt and a further attempt during report writing were both blocked, the latter at https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/target-size-minimum.html. Confirm those two numbers against w3.org before relying on them. The practical recommendation, which does not depend on the exact figure, is to design generously for older and less confident users and to keep destructive and non-destructive actions well separated.

## Don't build these, and here is the evidence to decline them with

**Live chat.** Roughly 50% of companies offer live chat and e-forums and **fewer than 2% of customers use them** ([McKinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/growth-marketing-and-sales/our-insights/overcoming-obstacles-to-digital-customer-care)). Nothing in the vendor material for small-ISP platforms ships live chat in the customer portal. For a one-person team it is worse than nothing, because an unstaffed chat widget sets a response expectation the portal then breaks. Build the callback request instead.

**Chatbots.** The research found **no reliable data on chatbot containment in telecom at all** — every result was vendor content asserting 70–90% with no methodology, which the researchers excluded as evidence rather than reporting as a benchmark. Worse, in this specific market it is an active negative: Flow reviewers single out **AI bots handling first contact** as a grievance ([Trustpilot](https://es.trustpilot.com/review/discoverflow.co)). Route to a human quickly.

**Native mobile apps.** Apps do score far better on satisfaction — J.D. Power puts ISP app login at 689 against an implied website 647, a 42-point gap ([Businesswire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260318404559/en/Telecom-Provider-Mobile-Apps-Deliver-Strong-Customer-Experience-but-Websites-Fall-Short-JD-Power-Finds)) — but that score is measured among the self-selected minority who installed one. In emerging markets app adoption is brutal: **My MTN leads Nigeria at 6.2%, MTN and Vodafone in Ghana sit around 5%, against roughly 25% for My Vodacom in South Africa**, with uptake tracking subscriber income ([allAfrica](https://allafrica.com/stories/202010010921.html)). That data is from 2020, Android-only, download-based, and the source itself notes downloads overstate real use. With Jamaica at roughly 62% Android and 38% iOS ([StatCounter](https://gs.statcounter.com/os-market-share/mobile/jamaica/2025) — period ambiguous, the rendered table was headed September 2026, so cite as recent rather than for a given year), two native apps would reach a single-digit share of the base while responsive web plus SMS reaches all of it. Splynx's white-label MyISP app is a genuine differentiator for Splynx; it is not a reason for ISPMan to build one. **No source gives a subscriber-count threshold at which an app becomes worthwhile** — any such number would be modelling, not research.

**TR-069/ACS router control.** This is the painful one, because the evidence says it is high value: customer-initiated reboot and Wi-Fi SSID/password change are the two controls that address the two commonest realities behind "my internet is slow" — a wedged CPE and neighbours on the Wi-Fi — without a truck roll, and Splynx is the only major platform shipping them ([Splynx](https://splynx.com/blog/customer-service/shift-towards-user-self-care-leveraging-splynx-customer-portal-tr-069-acs/)). Flow has had remote modem reset since 2018. But it requires an ACS and managed CPE, and where tenants' customers own their own routers it is unavailable regardless of what ISPMan builds. Note Centipid's honest framing of the same limitation: home Wi-Fi settings only where the ISP can reach the router remotely ([Centipid](https://docs.centipidbilling.com/customer-portal/overview)). Defer, and revisit only if a tenant standardises on managed CPE.

**Public outage maps.** A large-operator feature and an actively marketed product category of its own ([7Sigma NOC360 Sweep](https://www.accessnewswire.com/newsroom/en/telecommunications/7sigma-systems-launches-noc360-sweep-public-outage-map-1002390)). The scoped per-customer banner answers the question that drives the call; the map answers one nobody asked.

**Calendar-slot technician booking.** Shipped by large carriers like CenturyLink ([CenturyLink](https://www.centurylink.com/home/help/account/schedule-a-tech-appointment.html)) and sold as a dedicated field-service product. True slot booking needs committed field capacity per slot. The small-team version is a visit request with preferred windows, which is a ticket type.

**Usage analytics and plan-optimisation dashboards.** Only **16% of utility customers report getting value from proactive guidance, personalised usage data or bill-reduction strategies**, and only 33% say digital channels are straightforward to use at all ([KUBRA / J.D. Power](https://www.kubra.com/blog/elevating-digital-engagement-lessons-jd-powers-utility-study)). Features whose value depends on the customer caring about optimisation cluster in the low-value evidence; features that prevent a transaction failure cluster in the high-value evidence. On uncapped plans a consumption graph is close to decorative for billing purposes anyway — though **no source addresses the uncapped case directly**, so that reading is inference. Build session and uptime history instead, which costs the same and answers a question customers actually ask.

**Self-service address change.** **No ISP portal in the research documents it**, and for a WISP it implies a site survey and a line-of-sight check. It is a provisioning event, not a profile edit. Make it a request.

**In-portal payment reminders as an opt-in setting.** Used by **15%** of portal users ([US Bank 2020](https://www.usbank.com/dam/financialiq/documents/utilities-and-telecom-consumer-payment-insight-report-2020.pdf)). Same function, delivered as a default-on SMS, reaches everyone.

**Full offline-first sync, loyalty schemes, referral tracking.** No supporting usage evidence, and all of them consume the build capacity that tier one needs.

One trade-off worth naming before cutting calls becomes the only objective: across traditional remote channels, **upsell and cross-sell rates run almost nine times higher than across digital channels** ([McKinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/growth-marketing-and-sales/our-insights/overcoming-obstacles-to-digital-customer-care)). For a tenant ISP that currently sells upgrades on the phone, successful deflection has a revenue cost. That is an argument for putting the plan-upgrade path inside the portal where it can carry the offer, not an argument against deflection.

## Conclusion

The most useful thing this research establishes is a reordering. The instinct behind "payment is only one feature of a customer portal" is right, but the missing features are not the ones vendor feature matrices imply. Across eight platforms a consistent core five exists — view invoice, pay invoice, view usage, open and track a ticket, update contact details — and everything beyond it is differentiating rather than expected. What the measured evidence adds is that the differentiators worth having are the ones that resolve *service state*: is there an outage, is it mine, when does my service end, am I back on now that I have paid. Those are exactly the questions a billing platform with RADIUS, router reach and a subscriber-to-sector map can answer definitively and a national carrier's app cannot — Flow's reviewers complain about inaccurate balances and stale data, not missing screens. The competitive opening in Jamaica is data trustworthiness, and it is an integration problem, not a UI problem.

The second reordering is about where self-service actually happens. For a prepaid customer on a low-end Android phone who opens a portal ten times a year and pays cash at a counter, the portal is the ledger of record and the SMS is the channel. That inverts the usual build plan: the SMS queue, the walled garden and the reconnection automation deserve the engineering attention, while the portal's job is to be a fast, flat, trustworthy page that a tokenised link drops the customer into. Three gaps should stay visible as that gets built, because filling them later will change decisions. **No measured breakdown of prepaid self-care tasks exists anywhere in the research**, so the prepaid screen's design rests on reasoning. **No ISP publishes portal task analytics**, so instrument ISPMan's own portal from day one — within a year of multi-tenant data, the operator will hold better evidence on this question than any vendor has published. And **no source gives a subscriber threshold for app investment**, so let that decision follow observed smartphone and data-affordability behaviour in the tenants' actual subscriber bases rather than a headcount. On present evidence, responsive web plus SMS is not a compromise; it is the correct answer for this market.
